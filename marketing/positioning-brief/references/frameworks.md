# Frameworks

Positioning decides what is true and worth saying. Narrative decides the order in which to say it. Settle the positioning first; the same positioning can carry several narratives.

## Positioning lenses

Use the lenses that sharpen the answer. Not every offer needs all of them.

### Audience and trigger

Who gets the most value, fastest. Describe the segment, the role, and the moment that makes them look for a solution.

- B2B: kind of organisation, size, sector, the role that uses it, the role that decides, and what usually triggers the search.
- B2C: the situation and the need, not demographics alone.
- Name who it is not for. It keeps the message sharp.

### Value proposition canvas

Two sides that have to fit.

- Customer: jobs they are trying to get done (functional, social, emotional), pains that get in the way, and gains they hope for.
- Offer: the products and services, the pain relievers, and the gain creators.
- Fit: keep only the few relievers and creators that match the top pains and gains. The rest is a feature list.

### Jobs to be done

One sentence per job: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]." Good for finding the trigger and the words customers use.

### Alternatives and difference

1. What the audience does without this offer: a competitor, a spreadsheet, an agency, an in-house workaround, or nothing.
2. What only this offer has compared with those alternatives.
3. The value each difference creates for the customer.
4. The customers who care most about that value.
5. The frame of reference (the market category) that makes the value obvious.

Work in this order. Starting from the category tends to produce a me-too message.

### Positioning statement

For [audience] who [need or trigger], [offer] is a [category] that [key value]. Unlike [main alternative], it [difference], shown by [proof].

This is an internal sentence, not a tagline.

### Proof

Rank what you have, strongest first:

1. A measured result with a source.
2. A named customer who agreed to be named.
3. A product fact anyone can check, or a demo.
4. A third party: award, certification, analyst, press.
5. A plain claim. Say it carefully or leave it out.

## Narrative frameworks

Each framework is an order for the positioning content. The steps below are what the brief's arc holds.

### Strategic narrative

For launches, new categories, pitches, and anything bigger than a feature. Popularised by Andy Raskin.

1. Name a shift in the world: real, external, and already happening.
2. Show that it creates winners and losers.
3. Tease the promised land: the desirable future that is hard to reach alone.
4. Introduce the capabilities that overcome the obstacles on the way.
5. Present evidence that it works.

The customer reaching the promised land is the hero, not the product. "Our product exists" is not a shift.

### PAS: problem, agitate, solve

For a clear, felt pain with a direct fix.

1. Problem, in the customer's words.
2. Agitate: what it costs, and what happens if nothing changes.
3. Solve: the offer and the one mechanism that removes the problem.

Agitate only as far as the evidence goes, and in the brand's tone.

### BAB: before, after, bridge

For a transformation that is easy to show.

1. Before: the situation today.
2. After: the same situation, resolved.
3. Bridge: the offer is how you get from one to the other.

The after must be something the offer can deliver.

### ABT: and, but, therefore

For very short formats, openings, and one-liners.

1. And: the setup, facts the audience agrees with.
2. But: the conflict or the turn.
3. Therefore: the resolution and what to do.

The "but" carries the story. If it is weak, the piece is flat.

### AIDA: attention, interest, desire, action

For cold audiences who do not know the offer yet.

1. Attention: one image or line that stops them, tied to the message.
2. Interest: why it matters to them.
3. Desire: the value and the proof.
4. Action: one clear next step.

Attention from a gimmick that has nothing to do with the message is lost at the next step.

## Combining

A campaign can use a strategic narrative as its spine and a shorter framework for each piece: ABT for a teaser, PAS for an ad, BAB for a case story. The positioning underneath stays the same.
