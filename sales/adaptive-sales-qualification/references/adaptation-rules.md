# Adaptation Rules

## Purpose

Qualification effort should be proportional to the economic value, complexity, and risk of the sale.

## Mode Selection

### Lightweight

Use when most of the following are true:

- low deal value;
- short sales cycle;
- low acquisition cost;
- simple product;
- one or few decision participants;
- low implementation effort;
- reversible purchase;
- low regulatory and delivery risk.

Expected behavior:

- ask very few questions;
- tolerate moderate uncertainty;
- focus on fit, intent, affordability, and next action;
- avoid qualification effort that exceeds expected sales value.

### Standard

Use when several of the following are true:

- consultative SMB or mid-market sale;
- meaningful contract value;
- more than one stakeholder;
- moderate implementation effort;
- non-trivial integration or onboarding;
- identifiable procurement or approval process;
- moderate cost of sale.

Expected behavior:

- verify problem, value, urgency, process, stakeholders, fit, and economics;
- identify material unknowns;
- require a reciprocal next step;
- estimate probability using stage and evidence.

### Strategic

Use when any major factor is high:

- enterprise or public-sector sale;
- high cost of sale;
- long cycle;
- complex buying center;
- tender or formal procurement;
- security, legal, regulatory, or works-council review;
- material implementation or change effort;
- high delivery, reputational, or concentration risk;
- strong incumbent or competitive pressure;
- irreversible or long-term commitment.

Expected behavior:

- map the buying center;
- verify the decision and procurement process;
- require stronger evidence;
- evaluate competition and implementation risk;
- assess bid effort and opportunity cost;
- use explicit exit conditions.

## Qualification Intensity Principle

Required confidence increases with:

```text
deal value
+ remaining cost of sale
+ cycle duration
+ implementation complexity
+ decision irreversibility
+ delivery risk
+ regulatory exposure
+ competitive intensity
```

## Industry Adaptation

Retrieve or ask for:

- industry economics;
- common pains;
- buying cycles;
- typical stakeholders;
- regulatory constraints;
- procurement norms;
- acceptable proof;
- implementation dependencies;
- common reasons for delay or loss.

Do not assume that a signal means the same thing in every industry.

## Product Adaptation

Retrieve or ask for:

- product category;
- delivery model;
- implementation requirements;
- switching costs;
- time to value;
- pricing model;
- contract length;
- required integrations;
- user adoption requirements;
- customer risk if the purchase fails.

## Geography and Language Adaptation

Consider:

- contracting jurisdiction;
- local procurement conventions;
- required sales language;
- data residency;
- tax or invoicing requirements;
- cultural expectations around authority and consensus;
- local consumer or public-procurement rules.

Do not turn cultural patterns into stereotypes. Use them only as hypotheses to verify.

## B2C Adaptation

Use the model for B2C only when the purchase is high-consideration.

Focus on:

- need and suitability;
- affordability without exploiting vulnerability;
- decision participants;
- urgency;
- switching or cancellation constraints;
- consumer-protection requirements;
- next action.

Never use sensitive personal attributes or proxies for them.
